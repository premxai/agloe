When many LLM agents share a wiki, notes or memory, one agent's mistake can reach others simply by being copied, and after an incident operators ask who passed what to whom. We show that ordinary logs usually cannot say: they record what agents wrote, not what they read. In a real shared-wiki incident, even a perfect investigator could name the true source of at most 16% of copied behaviours, and the obvious detector ("the same string appeared twice") made 1,001 copy calls of which only 385 survive base-rate calibration. We prove ceilings on what any tracer can recover from log content, a bound on false copy calls under monoculture, and soundness of a gateway design in which every served copy carries its own load-bearing token: a trap street, after the fictitious streets mapmakers draw to catch copiers. We build a traceability audit, the gateway, and a benchmark with a hidden read log. In 7,265 offline agent runs of eight model families, tokens identify the immediate source for 70.5% of copying agents against 35.3% for the best simple edit-log rule, but the gain ranges from -1 to +68 points because some models re-derive access instead of copying links; closing that route raised the traceable share of the models that bypassed tokens from 25-50% to 100% with no loss of task completion. Tracing a planted bad tip cut the re-check list from 83% to 8% of outputs. We report what failed, including three flawed experiment designs and one hypothesis that was not met.
